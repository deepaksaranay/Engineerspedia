Stand in almost any building and every bit of weight above you — furniture, people, the roof, snow sitting on that roof — is being carried by something. It travels down through beams, into columns or walls, through the foundation, and finally into the soil or rock below. Engineers call this journey a load path, and tracing it is one of the first things a structural engineer does when looking at any building.

Two kinds of loads, working together

Structures are designed around two broad categories of load:

  • Dead loads are permanent — the weight of the building materials themselves: concrete floors, steel beams, brick walls, roofing.
  • Live loads are temporary and variable — people walking around, furniture, cars in a parking garage, snow, wind, and, in seismic zones, the shaking ground itself during an earthquake.

A structure has to be able to carry the worst realistic combination of these, not just the average day. That's why codes specify loads like "100 pounds per square foot" for an office floor even though most days it's far less — the design has to hold up on the day the conference room is packed and everyone brought a box of files.

What "load-bearing" actually means

A wall or column is "load-bearing" if it's part of the structural load path — remove it, and something above it loses its support. Non-load-bearing walls, by contrast, are just partitions; they divide space but carry only their own weight. This is why "can I knock down this wall?" is a structural question, not a decorating one — removing a load-bearing wall without adding something to replace its job (like a beam and new columns) can let the floor above sag or, in the worst case, collapse.

Rule of thumb: if a wall runs perpendicular to floor joists and sits above another wall or beam on the floor below, there's a good chance it's load-bearing. This isn't a substitute for an actual structural assessment — but it's why contractors get nervous about walls that "look important."

Following a load path, step by step

Picture a simple two-story house. Snow sits on the roof. Here's roughly where that weight goes:

  1. The roof sheathing and rafters carry the snow load to the walls or a ridge beam.
  2. Exterior walls (or interior load-bearing walls) carry that weight down to the floor framing below.
  3. Floor joists and beams carry it further down, often concentrating it onto specific posts or columns.
  4. Those columns carry it to the foundation walls or footings.
  5. The foundation spreads the load out and transfers it into the soil, which has to be strong enough not to compress or shift unevenly under that pressure.

If any link in that chain is missing, undersized, or damaged — a rotted post, a foundation crack, a wall removed without reinforcement — the load doesn't just disappear. It finds another path, often one the building wasn't designed for, which is how small problems turn into big ones over time.

Why shapes matter: trusses, arches, and triangles

Structural engineers lean heavily on triangles because triangles are geometrically rigid — unlike a rectangle, a triangle can't collapse into a different shape without one of its sides changing length. That's why roof trusses are built from triangles, and why cross-bracing (diagonal members forming triangles) shows up in everything from bridges to bookshelves. Arches work on a related principle: they convert vertical loads into compression that curves down through the arch itself, which is why ancient masonry arches, built with no steel reinforcement at all, are still standing after thousands of years.

Compression, tension, and why materials get chosen carefully

Members in a structure are typically in compression (being squeezed), tension (being stretched), or bending (a combination of both across their cross-section). Concrete is excellent in compression but weak in tension — which is why it's reinforced with steel rebar, a material that's excellent in tension, to handle the loads concrete alone can't. Steel beams are strong in both, which is part of why steel-framed buildings can span longer distances with thinner members than unreinforced masonry ever could.

A building doesn't need to be indestructible. It needs a complete, continuous path for every load it will realistically see, all the way to the ground.

Why this is worth understanding even outside civil engineering

Load path thinking generalizes surprisingly well. Any time you're evaluating a system — organizational, electrical, financial — asking "where does the stress actually go when things get overloaded, and what happens if one link in that chain fails?" is the same instinct structural engineers apply to buildings, just pointed at a different kind of structure.
